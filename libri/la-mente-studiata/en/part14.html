A.3 Convergences between traditions

The occasions on which distant traditions or disciplines arrive, by independent routes, at similar ideas or difficulties.

Idea AIdea BWhat they have in common
Rationalization as a defense (ch. 10, psychoanalytic tradition)Cognitive dissonance and self-justification (ch. 69, experimental social psychology)Both describe a mind that reconstructs acceptable reasons after a behavior; the two traditions reach the same idea with different methods, and the second makes it testable

Chapter 4 — The bare minimum of statistics

[FORMULA]

SD = √( Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1) )

Where:

• xᵢ — the value of each observation (for example, the number of words recalled by the i-th person)

• x̄ — the group mean

• n — the number of observations in the group

• Σ — the summation symbol: add up the term that follows over all observations, from 1 to n

What it means in plain words: for each person, you compute how far their score lies from the group mean (xᵢ − x̄); you square this deviation (to remove negative signs and keep positive and negative deviations from canceling each other out in the sum); you add up all the squared deviations; you divide by the number of observations minus one; finally you take the square root to return to the original unit of measurement (words, not "squared words").

Worked example: in a group of 4 people who recall 16, 18, 20 and 22 words respectively, the mean is 19. The deviations are −3, −1, 1, 3; squared: 9, 1, 1, 9; the sum is 20; divided by (4−1)=3 it gives 6.67; the square root is about 2.58. The standard deviation of this group is therefore about 2.58 words.

[FORMULA]

r = Σ[(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)] / √[Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²]

Where:

• xᵢ, yᵢ — the values of the two variables for each person (for example, hours of sleep and words recalled)

• x̄, ȳ — the means of variable x and variable y, respectively

• Σ — summation over all the people in the sample

What it means in plain words: the numerator becomes large and positive when people who are above the mean on one variable are also above the mean on the other (and likewise for those below); the denominator serves only to "normalize" the result, so that r always falls between −1 and +1, regardless of the units of the original variables. An r close to +1 indicates that the two variables increase together in an almost perfectly linear way; an r close to −1 indicates that as one increases the other tends to decrease; an r close to 0 indicates no linear relationship.

Worked example: five people sleep 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 hours and recall 14, 18, 16, 22 and 20 words respectively. The means are 7 hours and 18 words. The deviations from the mean are −2, −1, 0, +1, +2 for sleep and −4, 0, −2, +4, +2 for words; their products are 8, 0, 0, 4, 4, with a sum of 16. The sum of squared deviations is 10 for sleep and 40 for words. So r = 16 / √(10 · 40) = 16 / 20 = 0.80 — a strong positive relationship, but not a perfect one.

[FORMULA]

d = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SD_pooled

Where:

• x̄₁, x̄₂ — the means of the two groups being compared (for example, a caffeine group and a placebo group)

• SD_pooled — a weighted average of the standard deviations of the two groups, summarizing the internal variability of both

What it means in plain words: d expresses the difference between the two means not in the original units (words) but in standard deviation units — that is, how many "notches" of typical spread separate the two means. By rough convention, a d of around 0.2 is considered a small effect, around 0.5 medium, around 0.8 or above large — but these benchmarks should always be read in the specific context of the phenomenon studied, not as absolute thresholds.

Worked example: if the caffeine group recalls 20 words on average and the placebo group 18, with a pooled standard deviation of 4 words, then d = (20 − 18) / 4 = 0.5 — a medium-sized effect.

Chapter 15 — Classical conditioning

[FORMULA]

The prediction-error model of conditioning (Rescorla-Wagner).

ΔV = α · β · (λ − V)

Where:

• ΔV is the change, on a single trial, in the strength of the association between the conditioned stimulus and the outcome;

• α (alpha) is the salience of the conditioned stimulus — how noticeable it is, a value between 0 and 1;

• β (beta) is a parameter related to the strength of the outcome (the unconditioned stimulus), also between 0 and 1;

• λ (lambda) is the maximum associative strength that the outcome can support;

• V is the associative strength already held by the stimulus (and by any other stimuli present together with it) before this trial.

The term (λ − V) is the prediction error: the gap between what was expected (V) and the most the situation would allow one to expect (λ). As V approaches λ, the error shrinks and learning slows down until it stops — which is exactly why, in the blocking experiment described above, stimulus B learns nothing: the prediction error is already close to zero before B even appears, because A already predicted everything.

Worked example. Let α = 0.5 (a moderately salient stimulus), β = 0.6 (an outcome of intermediate strength), λ = 100 (the maximum possible associative strength for this outcome) and V = 40 (the association already acquired on previous trials). Then: ΔV = 0.5 × 0.6 × (100 − 40) = 0.3 × 60 = 18. Associative strength increases by 18 points on this trial, bringing V to 58 for the next trial — an increment that will grow smaller and smaller as V approaches 100, tracing the typical acquisition curve that slows down without ever stopping abruptly.

Chapter 20 — The neuron as a signaling unit

[FORMULA]

The Nernst equation makes it possible to calculate the equilibrium potential of a single ion from its concentrations inside and outside the cell:

E_ion = (RT / zF) · ln([ion]out / [ion]in)

Where:

• E_ion is the equilibrium potential of the ion, expressed in volts (or millivolts).

• R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J/(mol·K)) — how much thermal energy is available per mole of substance at a given temperature.

• T is the absolute temperature in kelvin (for an organism at 37 °C, T = 310 K).

• z is the valence of the ion (its electric charge: +1 for K⁺ and Na⁺, +2 for Ca²⁺, −1 for Cl⁻).

• F is the Faraday constant (96,485 coulombs/mol) — the electric charge carried by one mole of elementary charges.

• ln is the natural logarithm.

• [ion]out and [ion]in are the concentrations of the ion outside and inside the cell.

At body temperature, the term RT/F, converted to millivolts and with the natural logarithm rewritten as a base-10 logarithm (by multiplying by 2.303), simplifies to a practical constant of about 61.5 mV for a monovalent ion (z = 1). For quick use, the formula therefore becomes:

E_ion ≈ (61.5 mV / z) · log₁₀([ion]out / [ion]in)

Worked example — the equilibrium potential of potassium. Typical values for a mammalian neuron are [K⁺]in ≈ 140 millimolar (mM) and [K⁺]out ≈ 5 mM, with z = +1 (potassium carries a single positive charge). Substituting:

E_K = 61.5 mV · log₁₀(5 / 140) = 61.5 mV · log₁₀(0.0357) = 61.5 mV · (−1.447) ≈ −89 mV

This number — close to the resting potential values actually measured in real neurons, typically between −65 and −70 mV — shows why the resting potential is dominated by potassium: if the K⁺ leak channels were the only channels open at rest, the cell would settle almost exactly at this value. The fact that the real resting potential (about −70 mV) is slightly less negative than −89 mV indicates that a small amount of Na⁺ also continually leaks into the cell, shifting the balance toward somewhat more positive values — consistent with the fact that the equilibrium potential of Na⁺, calculated from its typical concentrations ([Na⁺]out ≈ 145 mM, [Na⁺]in ≈ 15 mM), turns out to be positive: E_Na = 61.5 mV · log₁₀(145/15) ≈ +61 mV — very far from the resting potential, and for that very reason capable of producing a dramatic change when Na⁺ channels open en masse, as in the next section.

Chapter 27 — Genes, environment and their interaction

[FORMULA]

A direct way — albeit simplified compared with more modern statistical methods — to estimate heritability from a twin study is Falconer's formula:

h² = 2 · (r_MZ − r_DZ)

Where:

• h² is the estimate of narrow-sense heritability (the proportion of variance in the trait, in the population studied, that is statistically associated with additive genetic factors).

• r_MZ is the correlation coefficient (in the sense already defined in chapter 4) between the scores of the two twins, calculated over pairs of monozygotic twins.

• r_DZ is the same correlation coefficient, calculated over pairs of same-sex dizygotic twins.

Worked example. Suppose that, for a psychological trait measured with a standardized test, the correlation between the scores of monozygotic twins turns out to be r_MZ = 0.70, and that between dizygotic twins r_DZ = 0.40. Applying the formula:

h² = 2 · (0.70 − 0.40) = 2 · 0.30 = 0.60

The result is interpreted as follows: about 60% of the observed variance in that trait, in that population, is statistically associated with additive genetic differences between people; the remaining 40% is associated with environmental factors (shared and non-shared between the two twins) and with measurement error. The intuitive reasoning behind the formula is that, if a trait were determined entirely by genes, monozygotic twins (100% shared DNA) should be almost identical (r_MZ close to 1), while dizygotic twins should resemble each other only as much as any two siblings; the larger the gap between the two correlations, the more that gap — attributable only to the greater genetic similarity of monozygotic twins, given that both types of twins typically share the same family environment — is explained by genes.

Chapter 28 — From transduction to sensation

[FORMULA]

Weber's law formalizes this proportionality:

ΔI / I = k

Where:

• ΔI is the just noticeable difference, that is, the smallest detectable increase in intensity.

• I is the intensity of the reference stimulus.

• k is the Weber fraction, a constant specific to each sensory modality (not a universal value): about 0.02 for weight discrimination, about 0.08 for brightness, about 0.05 for loudness.

Worked example. With k = 0.02 (weight discrimination) and a reference weight I = 200 g, the just noticeable difference is:

ΔI = k · I = 0.02 · 200 g = 4 g

So at least 4 grams of difference are needed to notice that a second weight is heavier than the first. But with a reference weight ten times larger, I = 2000 g, the same constant k = 0.02 requires:

ΔI = 0.02 · 2000 g = 40 g

Not 4 grams as in the previous case: the difference needed has grown in proportion to the baseline rather than staying fixed. This is exactly the phenomenon the law describes.

[FORMULA]

Fechner's law:

S = k · ln(I / I₀)

Where:

• S is the subjective intensity of the sensation.

• I is the physical intensity of the stimulus.

• I₀ is the intensity at the absolute threshold, the point at which the sensation begins to exist.

• k is a constant specific to the sensory modality.

• ln is the natural logarithm.

The practical consequence is that sensation grows more slowly than physical intensity: doubling the brightness of a lamp does not double its perceived brightness, because the relationship is logarithmic, not linear.

Worked example. Let k = 1 and I₀ = 1 (arbitrary units, chosen only to isolate the shape of the function). A physical intensity I = 10 (ten times the threshold) corresponds to a sensation of:

S = 1 · ln(10 / 1) = ln(10) ≈ 2.30

Raising the physical intensity to I = 100 — ten times more intense than before, one hundred times the threshold — the sensation becomes:

S = 1 · ln(100 / 1) = ln(100) ≈ 4.61

A tenfold increase in physical intensity (from 10 to 100) has produced an increase in sensation of exactly double (from 2.30 to 4.61, because ln 100 = 2 · ln 10), not tenfold: this is exactly the gap between physical growth and subjective effect that the formula describes. An increase that is enormous in absolute physical terms (from a light bulb to a floodlight) produces a much more modest increase in sensation than the physical increase would suggest — one of the reasons why scales such as decibels for sound or f-stops for photographic exposure are themselves logarithmic: they are built to match how the perceptual system actually responds, not how physical energy is distributed.

Chapter 34 — The first experiments on memory

[FORMULA]

An approximate but useful description of the forgetting curve is an exponential decay:

R(t) = e^(−t / S)

Where:

• R(t) is the proportion of material retained (measured, for example, through savings) at time t after the original learning.

• t is the time elapsed since learning, in any consistent unit (here: days).

• S is the strength of the memory trace, a parameter representing how stably the information was encoded: the larger S is, the slower the decay.

• e is the base of natural logarithms (≈2.718).

Worked example. With a trace of strength S = 1 day (weak learning, such as a list read only once), after t = 1 day the proportion retained is:

R(1) = e^(−1/1) = e^(−1) ≈ 0.37

Just over a third remains after a single day. After t = 3 days, with the same trace strength:

R(3) = e^(−3/1) = e^(−3) ≈ 0.05

About 5% remains after three days: the bulk of the loss has already occurred in the first twenty-four hours, a pattern the model captures well over the first day. The exponential model, however, describes only the initial drop well: in real data the tail is slower, and a power function fits better over the long term. More robust learning (obtained, for example, by repeating the material several times or by spacing the repetitions over time) corresponds to a higher value of S, which lengthens the tail of the curve without changing its overall shape.

Chapter 44 — Decision under risk

[FORMULA]

The value function of prospect theory has a shape that can be approximated, separately for gains and for losses, as:

v(x) = x^α for x ≥ 0 (gains)

v(x) = −λ · (−x)^β for x < 0 (losses)

Where:

• x is the size of the gain or loss relative to the reference point.

• v(x) is the subjective value attributed to that gain or loss.

• α and β are exponents smaller than 1 (typical estimates around 0.88), which make the function concave for gains and convex for losses: the subjective difference between gaining 100 and 200 euros is perceived as larger than the difference between gaining 1100 and 1200 euros, and the same holds, in mirror image, for losses.

• λ is the loss aversion coefficient, typically estimated at around 2 (with values between 1.5 and 2.5 depending on the study): a loss has a negative subjective impact greater than the subjective pleasure of a gain of the same absolute size.

Worked example. With α = β = 0.88 and λ = 2.25, a gain of 100 euros produces a subjective value v(100) = 100^0.88 ≈ 57.5. A loss of 100 euros produces a subjective value v(−100) = −2.25 · 100^0.88 ≈ −129.5. In absolute value, the loss (129.5) weighs more than twice as much as the gain of the same size (57.5): this is exactly why most people turn down an even double-or-nothing bet (50% chance of winning 100 euros, 50% chance of losing 100), even though the expected monetary value is zero — in terms of subjective value according to this model, the expected value of the bet is negative: 0.5 · 57.5 + 0.5 · (−129.5) ≈ −36.0 (in simplified form, with undistorted probabilities; the full theory uses decision weights that change the result slightly, without changing the conclusion).

Chapter 45 — Probability and formal reasoning

[FORMULA]

Bayes' theorem makes it possible to calculate the correct probability:

P(M|+) = [P(+|M) · P(M)] / [P(+|M) · P(M) + P(+|¬M) · P(¬M)]

Where:

• P(M|+) is the probability of being ill, given a positive test result (what we want to calculate).

• P(+|M) is the sensitivity of the test, the probability of a positive result given that one is ill (0.90).

• P(M) is the base rate of the disease in the population (0.01).

• P(+|¬M) is the false positive rate, the probability of a positive result given that one is not ill (1 − specificity = 0.09).

• P(¬M) is the probability of not being ill (1 − 0.01 = 0.99).

Worked example. In a hypothetical population of 10,000 people: 100 are ill (1%), of whom the test correctly detects 90 (sensitivity 90%). Of the 9,900 healthy people, 9% (891 people) nonetheless test positive by mistake (false positives). The total number of positive tests is therefore 90 + 891 = 981. Of these 981 positives, only 90 are actually ill:

P(M|+) = 90 / 981 ≈ 0.092

The real probability of being ill given a positive test is about 9%, not the 90% that intuition suggests — almost ten times lower. The reason is that, since the disease is rare, the absolute number of false positives generated by a much larger healthy population far exceeds the number of true positives generated by the small ill population, even with a fairly accurate test. Ignoring the base rate of the disease (the initial 1%) leads to a drastic overestimate of the real probability.

Chapter 52 — Motivation

[FORMULA]

A more general version of this principle, developed in computational reinforcement learning, extends the prediction error to chains of events spread out over time, not just to a single stimulus-outcome pairing:

δ_t = r_t + γ · V(s_{t+1}) − V(s_t)

Where:

• δ_t is the prediction error at time t (the signal that, in the brain, corresponds to the phasic activity of dopaminergic neurons).

• r_t is the reward actually received at time t.

• V(s_t) is the estimated value of the state one is in at time t, that is, how much future reward is expected starting from that state.

• V(s_{t+1}) is the estimated value of the next state.

• γ (gamma) is a discount factor between 0 and 1 that reduces the weight of future rewards relative to immediate ones.

Worked example. Consider a monkey learning that a light (state s_t, estimated value V(s_t)=0.2) reliably precedes a reward of fruit juice (r_t=1) with no further relevant intermediate states (V(s_{t+1})=0), with γ=0.9. At the moment of the reward: δ_t = 1 + 0.9·0 − 0.2 = 0.8. The positive signal, large relative to a reward of 1, reflects that the reward was much greater than the estimated value of the state predicted: the animal still has a lot to learn. With repeated learning, V(s_t) progressively approaches 1 and δ_t approaches zero: the reward becomes fully predicted, and — as shown by the recordings of dopaminergic neurons in chapter 15 — the dopamine signal at the reward itself disappears, shifting instead to the predictive stimulus (the light) at the moment it first appears.

Chapter 54 — Studying a subject who cannot speak

[FORMULA]

For each infant, the looking proportion toward stimulus A is calculated:

P = T_A / (T_A + T_B)

Where:

• P is the looking proportion toward stimulus A, a number between 0 and 1;

• T_A is the total time (in seconds) the infant spent looking at stimulus A;

• T_B is the total time (in seconds) the infant spent looking at stimulus B.

If the infant does not distinguish the two stimuli and has no preference, P = 0.5 is expected. To find out whether the group of infants prefers A, the mean of the individual P values is compared with 0.5 using a one-sample t test: t = (P̄ − 0.5) / (s / √n), where P̄ is the mean of the proportions, s their standard deviation and n the number of infants.

Worked example. An infant looks at A for 14 seconds and at B for 6: P = 14/(14 + 6) = 0.70. With n = 16 infants, a mean P̄ = 0.62 and a standard deviation s = 0.15, the standard error is 0.15/√16 = 0.0375 and t = (0.62 − 0.5)/0.0375 = 3.2. With 15 degrees of freedom this value corresponds to a probability of about 0.006 that a mean this far from 0.5 would arise by chance if the infants looked at the two stimuli indifferently. Remember, as seen in chapter 4, that the p-value tells you how surprising the data would be if there were no preference at all, not how large or important the preference itself is.

Chapter 59 — Language in development

[FORMULA]

The transitional probability between two syllables A and B measures how much B tends to follow A:

P(B|A) = frequency(AB) / frequency(A)

Where:

• P(B|A) is the probability that syllable A is followed by syllable B;

• frequency(AB) is the number of times that, in the stream, A is immediately followed by B;

• frequency(A) is the total number of occurrences of A.

Worked example. In the stream used in the experiment, the syllable “bi” appears 45 times, and in all 45 it is followed by “da” (part of the same word): P(da|bi) = 45/45 = 1.0. The syllable “ku”, the last of a word, appears 45 times and is followed 15 times by “pa” (the beginning of another word), 15 by another initial syllable and 15 by a third: P(pa|ku) = 15/45 ≈ 0.33. A drop in probability from 1.0 to 0.33 signals a word boundary: it is the kind of cue that infants seem to use.

Chapter 61 — Describing differences

[FORMULA]

In the simplest model, with a single factor, the correlation between two questions i and j is the product of their factor loadings:

r_ij = λ_i · λ_j

Where:

• r_ij is the observed correlation between question i and question j;

• λ_i (lambda) is the factor loading of question i: the correlation between question i and the unobserved factor;

• λ_j is the factor loading of question j, defined in the same way;

• λ_i² is the share of the variability of question i explained by the common factor; the rest is specific to that question, or is measurement error.

Worked example. Suppose that the loadings of the three questions in the example above are λ₁ = 0.8, λ₂ = 0.7 and λ₃ = 0.6. The model predicts the correlations: r₁₂ = 0.8 · 0.7 = 0.56; r₁₃ = 0.8 · 0.6 = 0.48; r₂₃ = 0.7 · 0.6 = 0.42. The share of variability explained by the factor is λ² = 0.64, 0.49 and 0.36 for the three questions. The real procedure goes in the opposite direction: the three correlations are observed and the loadings are derived from them. With three questions a single step is enough: λ₁² = (r₁₂ · r₁₃) / r₂₃ = (0.56 · 0.48) / 0.42 = 0.64, from which λ₁ = 0.8. With hundreds of adjectives the calculation is the same in spirit, but it requires a computer.

Chapter 62 — Stability and change

[FORMULA]

The correction for attenuation estimates the correlation that would be observed between the two scores if they were free of measurement error:

r_true = r_observed / √(r_xx · r_yy)

Where:

• r_observed is the correlation actually measured between the first and the second measurement;

• r_xx is the reliability of the first measurement (for example its short-interval test-retest reliability);

• r_yy is the reliability of the second measurement;

• √ denotes the square root of the product of the two reliabilities.

Worked example. A conscientiousness scale is administered at age twenty and again at thirty, and the correlation is 0.60. The reliability of the scale, measured over a short interval, is 0.80 on both occasions. Then: r_true = 0.60 / √(0.80 · 0.80) = 0.60 / 0.80 = 0.75. Part of the observed “instability” (from 1.0 to 0.60) is therefore measurement error; the rest, from 1.0 to 0.75, is genuine change, or the effect of other sources of difference between the two occasions.

Chapter 63 — The person-situation debate

[FORMULA]

The Spearman-Brown formula estimates the reliability of a measure obtained as the average of k observations, starting from the reliability of a single observation:

ρ_k = k · ρ / (1 + (k − 1) · ρ)

Where:

• ρ_k (rho) is the reliability of the average of k observations;

• ρ is the reliability of a single observation, that is, the expected correlation between two observations of the same kind;

• k is the number of observations averaged (days, situations or questions).

Worked example. Suppose that the correlation between behavior on one day and behavior on another day is 0.20, a plausible value for a single behavior. With k = 10 days: ρ₁₀ = 10 · 0.20 / (1 + 9 · 0.20) = 2.0 / 2.8 ≈ 0.71. With k = 20 days: ρ₂₀ = 20 · 0.20 / (1 + 19 · 0.20) = 4.0 / 4.8 ≈ 0.83. The same trait, which predicts little on a single day, is measured much more reliably when averaged over twenty days. The gain diminishes as k grows.

Chapter 64 — Intelligence

[FORMULA]

The standardized IQ score is obtained in two steps. First, the raw score is transformed into a z-score, which indicates how many standard deviations from the mean the person lies:

z = (x − μ) / σ

Then it is converted to the conventional IQ scale:

IQ = 100 + 15 · z

Where:

• x is the person's raw score (for example the number of correct answers);

• μ (mu) is the mean of the raw scores in the reference sample;

• σ (sigma) is the standard deviation of the raw scores in the same sample;

• z is the standardized score: 0 if x equals the mean, positive if it is above, negative if it is below;

• 100 and 15 are the mean and standard deviation chosen by convention for the IQ scale.

Worked example. In a reference sample the mean is 50 correct answers and the standard deviation is 8. A person answers 62 questions correctly: z = (62 − 50) / 8 = 1.5; IQ = 100 + 15 · 1.5 = 122.5, that is, about 123. If the distribution is bell-shaped, a score with z = 1.5 exceeds about 93% of the reference sample.

Chapter 65 — Measuring personality

[FORMULA]

Standardized alpha estimates the reliability of a scale from the number of questions and their average correlation:

α = k · r̄ / (1 + (k − 1) · r̄)

Where:

• α (alpha) is the index of internal consistency, between 0 and 1;

• k is the number of questions in the scale;

• r̄ (r-bar) is the average correlation between pairs of questions in the scale.

Worked example. A six-question scale, in which the average correlation between two questions is 0.30: α = 6 · 0.30 / (1 + 5 · 0.30) = 1.8 / 2.5 = 0.72. With twelve questions of the same quality: α = 12 · 0.30 / (1 + 11 · 0.30) = 3.6 / 4.3 ≈ 0.84. The formula is the same as the Spearman-Brown formula in chapter 63: averaging more questions is like averaging more occasions.

Chapter 66 — When the situation is stronger than disposition

[FORMULA]

The effect of a condition on the probability of helping can be expressed with the odds ratio, which compares the probabilities in two groups while taking into account how far they are from the extremes:

OR = [p₁ / (1 − p₁)] / [p₂ / (1 − p₂)]

Where:

• p₁ is the proportion of people who help in the comparison condition (no hurry);

• p₂ is the proportion of people who help in the other condition (great hurry);

• p / (1 − p) is the odds: the ratio between the probability of helping and that of not helping;

• OR equals 1 if the two conditions do not differ, and is greater than 1 the more the first condition favors helping.

Worked example. With p₁ = 0.63 and p₂ = 0.10: odds in the no-hurry condition = 0.63 / 0.37 ≈ 1.70; odds in the hurry condition = 0.10 / 0.90 ≈ 0.11. The ratio is OR ≈ 1.70 / 0.11 ≈ 15. For those not in a hurry, the relative odds of helping rather than not helping are about fifteen times higher. An effect this large in a small sample should be read with the caution of chapter 5: effects estimated on few participants tend to shrink in replications.

Chapter 67 — Conformity

[FORMULA]

If every person had the same probability p of conforming on each trial, and the trials were independent, the probability of conforming at least once over n critical trials would be:

P(at least once) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ

Where:

• p is the probability of conforming on a single critical trial;

• 1 − p is the probability of answering independently on that trial;

• n is the number of critical trials;

• (1 − p)ⁿ is the probability of never conforming on any of the n trials, if the trials are independent.

Worked example. With p = 0.36 and n = 12: (1 − 0.36)¹² = 0.64¹² ≈ 0.005, so P(at least once) ≈ 1 − 0.005 = 0.995, that is, about 99.5%. The value Asch observed, however, is 74%. The difference is not an error in the study: it tells us that people are not all alike. If some never conform and others conform almost always, the average probability of one third describes no one in particular, and the assumption that everyone has the same p collapses.

Chapter 68 — Obedience

[FORMULA]

An obedience rate of 65% among 40 people is an estimate, and it should be accompanied by its uncertainty. The standard error of a proportion measures how much the observed proportion can vary purely because of sampling:

SE = √[ p̂ · (1 − p̂) / n ]

From which an approximate 95% confidence interval follows (as in chapter 4): p̂ ± 1.96 · SE

Where:

• p̂ (p-hat) is the proportion observed in the sample;

• n is the number of participants;

• SE is the standard error;

• 1.96 is the value that leaves out 5% of cases, half on each side, in a bell-shaped distribution.

Worked example. With p̂ = 26/40 = 0.65 and n = 40: SE = √(0.65 · 0.35 / 40) = √0.00569 ≈ 0.075. The interval is 0.65 ± 1.96 · 0.075 = 0.65 ± 0.15, that is, from about 0.50 to 0.80. Obedience in that condition, in the population the 40 came from, plausibly lies between 50% and 80%. For the variant in the commercial office (19 out of 40, 47.5%) the interval is about 0.32-0.63: the two intervals overlap substantially, which explains why the 17.5-point difference is not significant (a direct comparison of the two proportions gives the same result: z ≈ 1.6, p ≈ 0.11).

Chapter 69 — Cognitive dissonance and self-justification

[FORMULA]

Festinger proposed, as a conceptual model rather than a direct measure, that the magnitude of dissonance depends on the ratio of dissonant cognitions to the total, each weighted by its importance:

D = Σ (importance of dissonant) / [Σ (importance of dissonant) + Σ (importance of consonant)]

Where:

• D is the magnitude of dissonance, between 0 (none) and 1 (maximum);

• Σ denotes the sum over the cognitions of that type;

• dissonant cognitions are those that contradict the behavior or belief in question; consonant cognitions are those that support it.

Worked example (with illustrative values, assigned by the author to show the logic). A person has told a lie: the dissonant cognition (“I lied”) has importance 4. In condition A there is one consonant cognition, “they paid me one dollar”, with importance 1: D = 4 / (4 + 1) = 0.80. In condition B the consonant cognition is “they paid me twenty dollars”, with importance 5: D = 4 / (4 + 5) ≈ 0.44. For the same lie, a high payment reduces dissonance, and a low payment leaves it high. The formula was never a practical measure: “importances” cannot be measured in common units, which is why the theory was tested with experiments and not with this equation.

Chapter 70 — Persuasion and attitude change

[FORMULA]

When two groups are compared on a dichotomous outcome (votes / does not vote), the difference between the proportions has a standard error:

SE = √[ p₁(1 − p₁)/n₁ + p₂(1 − p₂)/n₂ ]

Where:

• p₁ and p₂ are the proportions in the two groups (for example, the percentage who vote in the group that received the message and in the group that did not);

• n₁ and n₂ are the sizes of the two groups;

• SE is the measure of how much the observed difference can vary purely because of sampling. The ratio z = (p₁ − p₂) / SE indicates how many standard errors the difference lies from zero; beyond about 2, the difference is called statistically significant.

Worked example (with illustrative values). With n₁ = n₂ = 600,000, p₁ = 0.604 and p₂ = 0.600: SE = √[0.604 · 0.396/600,000 + 0.600 · 0.400/600,000] = √(3.99 · 10⁻⁷ + 4.00 · 10⁻⁷) ≈ 0.00089. The difference is 0.004 (four tenths of a percentage point) and z = 0.004 / 0.00089 ≈ 4.5: significant, even though the difference is tiny. With groups of 600 people instead of 600,000, SE would be about 32 times larger (√1000; about 0.028) and the same difference would give z ≈ 0.14: not significant. With enormous samples, even extremely small effects turn out to be statistically significant; how much they matter in practice is another question, and concerns effect size.

Chapter 71 — Us and them

[FORMULA]

Effect sizes are sometimes reported as a correlation r, sometimes as a standardized difference d (chapter 4). To compare studies that use different measures, one is converted into the other:

d = 2r / √(1 − r²)

Where:

• r is the correlation between contact and prejudice (negative if more contact means less prejudice);

• r² is the square of the correlation, the share of shared variability;

• d is the difference between groups expressed in standard deviations, in the sense of chapter 4.

Worked example. With r = −0.20 (the order of magnitude cited for the meta-analysis on contact): d = 2 · (−0.20) / √(1 − 0.04) = −0.40 / 0.98 ≈ −0.41. In other words, people with more contact have, on average, prejudice about four tenths of a standard deviation lower: a small-to-moderate effect by the conventional categories of chapter 4. With r² = 0.04, contact shares 4% of the variability in prejudice.

Chapter 72 — Helping, aggression, the group

[FORMULA]

The fact that each individual acts less when there are more witnesses does not mean that the emergency more often goes without help. If each witness acted independently with probability p, the probability that at least one of n witnesses acts would be:

P(at least one) = 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ

Where:

• p is the probability that a single witness acts;

• 1 − p is the probability that they do not act;

• n is the number of witnesses;

• (1 − p)ⁿ is the probability that no one acts, if the decisions are independent.

Worked example. A lone witness acts with p = 0.85: P = 0.85. With five witnesses, each with p = 0.31 (the value from the experiment with more people present): P = 1 − 0.69⁵ = 1 − 0.156 ≈ 0.84. If the decisions were independent, the probability that at least one helps would be almost the same as that of a lone witness (0.84 versus 0.85), even though each individual acts much less. But independence is precisely what the experiment calls into question: in the processes of diffusion of responsibility and pluralistic ignorance everyone waits for the others, and the decisions are linked, so the real probability that at least one acts may be lower than the calculated value.

Chapter 74 — How a classification is built

[FORMULA]

κ = (p₀ − pₑ) / (1 − pₑ)

Where:

• κ (kappa) is the agreement index, which equals 1 with perfect agreement, 0 with agreement equal to that expected by chance, and negative values with agreement below chance;

• p₀ is the observed proportion of cases in which the two raters give the same answer;

• pₑ is the proportion of agreement expected by chance: the sum of the products of the frequencies with which each rater gave that answer (the probability that both say “yes” by chance, plus the probability that both say “no”);

• 1 − pₑ is the maximum possible agreement beyond chance.

Worked example. Out of 100 people, the two clinicians both say “yes” in 40 cases, both say “no” in 35, and disagree on the remaining 25 (the first says yes and the second no in 10, the reverse in 15). The observed agreement is p₀ = (40 + 35) / 100 = 0.75. The first clinician says “yes” 50% of the time (40 + 10), the second 55% (40 + 15), so pₑ = 0.50 × 0.55 + 0.50 × 0.45 = 0.275 + 0.225 = 0.50. Then κ = (0.75 − 0.50) / (1 − 0.50) = 0.50. An observed agreement of 75% corresponds to a kappa of 0.50, that is, halfway between chance and perfect agreement.

Chapter 75 — Explanatory models

[FORMULA]

Relative risk: RR = R_exposed / R_unexposed

Expected risk with two independent factors: R_expected = R₀ × RR_A × RR_B

Where:

• R_exposed and R_unexposed are the proportions of people who develop the disorder among those who have the factor and those who do not;

• RR (relative risk) tells how many times the factor multiplies the risk (it equals 1 if it does not change it);

• R₀ is the risk for those who have neither of the two factors;

• RR_A and RR_B are the relative risks of the two factors assessed on their own;

• R_expected is the risk that would be observed for those who have both if the two factors acted independently, each multiplying on its own.

Worked example (made-up values for illustration). The risk for those with neither vulnerability nor stress is R₀ = 5%. With vulnerability alone it is 6% (RR_A = 6 / 5 = 1.2), with stress alone 10% (RR_B = 10 / 5 = 2.0). If they acted independently, those with both would have R_expected = 5% × 1.2 × 2.0 = 12%. If the data show 30%, the combination weighs much more than the sum of its parts: there is an interaction. The same holds with an additive calculation (5% + 1 point + 5 points = 11%, versus the observed 30%). The empirical question is always whether real data show a discrepancy of this kind and whether it is replicable.

Chapter 76 — What works in therapy and how we know it

[FORMULA]

NNT = 1 / (R_treatment − R_control)

Where:

• NNT is the number of people who need to be treated for, on average, one more of them to achieve the favorable outcome than would without the treatment;

• R_treatment is the proportion of people with the favorable outcome (for example a reduction of at least half of the symptoms) in the treated group;

• R_control is the same proportion in the control group;

• the difference R_treatment − R_control is the absolute risk reduction, or absolute benefit.

Worked example. If 50% of those treated respond and 30% of controls do, the difference is 0.50 − 0.30 = 0.20, and NNT = 1 / 0.20 = 5. Five people must be treated for one more to respond. This does not mean that one person in five benefits from the treatment and the others do not: out of ten people treated, five respond, of whom three would have responded anyway (like the 30% of controls) and two respond thanks to the treatment, while the other five do not respond despite the treatment; two additional responses for every ten treated equal one for every five. The value depends on how “response” is defined and on the baseline risk, and it changes with the threshold chosen.

Chapter 77 — Neurodiversity: when the category itself is in question

[FORMULA]

N = 1,000 / p

Where:

• p is the prevalence expressed as the number of cases per 1,000 children;

• N is the number of children among whom, on average, one case is expected (expressed as “one in N”);

Chapter 79 — What experiments on consciousness measure
